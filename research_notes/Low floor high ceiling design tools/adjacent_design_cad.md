## Key Question 1 — Architecture: Rhino + Grasshopper, Revit/BIM (and Dynamo), SketchUp, Archicad, Vectorworks. Is Grasshopper the best existing example of "elementary use is structurally the professional system"?

### Takeaway
Rhino + Grasshopper is the strongest architecture-side example of structural continuity: the student seat (US$195, one-time) is the identical, perpetual, commercially usable program as the US$995 professional seat, Grasshopper ships inside it, and the design-education literature shows novices succeed with the visual dataflow while experts hit a documented ceiling (perceptual limits of visual algorithms; models that break under change) that is escaped by scripting inside the same environment. Revit, Archicad and Vectorworks all give students the full professional program free but fence it with watermarks, non-commercial terms or one-year renewals; SketchUp has the lowest floor but its free tier is a different, reduced product (web-only, no extensions/LayOut/DWG), which is where its plateau comes from.

Grasshopper as a learning system — evidence
- Celani & Vaz (2012, International Journal of Architectural Computing), "CAD Scripting and Visual Programming Languages for Implementing Computational Design Concepts: A Comparison from a Pedagogical Point of View": "with novice students better results were obtained with the visual programming language"; visual tools were used mainly for parametric variation and randomness, while "scripting ... was used by advanced students to implement rule-based generative systems"; conclusion: "visual languages can be very useful for making architecture students understand general programming concepts, but scripting languages are fundamental for implementing generative design systems" — [CumInCAD record](https://papers.cumincad.org/cgi-bin/works/paper/ijac201210108); [PDF](https://papers.cumincad.org/data/works/att/ijac201210108.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/CAD-Scripting-and-Visual-Programming-Languages-for-Celani-Vaz/cc8d669d622b6f9f5043eb187a97c4826d50e4cf)
- A later teaching study ("Teaching parametric design: fostering algorithmic thinking through incomplete recipes", Open House International) summarises Celani & Vaz and a companion comparison as concluding that "since visual construction of algorithms are perceptually limited, they are more successful in relatively simple and pedagogical uses" — [Emerald / Open House International](https://www.emerald.com/ohi/article/49/4/736/1212363/Teaching-parametric-design-fostering-algorithmic)
- Aish & Woodbury (2005), "Multi-level Interaction in Parametric Design" (Smart Graphics, LNCS 3638, pp. 151–162): parametric systems "model a design as a constrained collection of schemata"; "designers work in such systems at two levels: definition of schemata and constraints; and search within a schema collection for meaningful instances"; the paper argues for multiple views of the constraint model, refined with "a large group of architecture practitioners" and realised in Bentley's GenerativeComponents — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/11536482_13); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Multi-level-Interaction-in-Parametric-Design-Aish-Woodbury/6175b107af0eb4d178dd642a618c4c2ef9d50bf2); Woodbury, "Whither design space?" (AI EDAM 2006) — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1017/S0890060406060057)
- Daniel Davis, PhD thesis (RMIT, 2013), "Modelled on Software Engineering: Flexible Parametric Models in the Practice of Architecture": parametric models in practice "often can't absorb certain design changes"; changing a model's functions or parameters can break it, leaving the designer to rebuild or abandon the change; there is "no agreed definition or measure of flexibility"; case studies Sagrada Família, Dermoid pavilion, Responsive Acoustic Surface; conclusion that architects and software engineers face similar problems answerable with similar methods — [danieldavis.com/thesis](https://danieldavis.com/thesis); [Chapter 1](https://www.danieldavis.com/thesis-ch1/); [Chapter 4](https://danieldavis.com/thesis-ch4); [full PDF](https://www.danieldavis.com/papers/danieldavis_thesis.pdf)
- "Design of Dataflow" (Nexus Network Journal, 2014) is an academic treatment of dataflow (Grasshopper-style) design environments (title only retrieved) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-014-0222-8); Wikipedia's Grasshopper 3D article cites Celani & Vaz — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grasshopper_3D)
- Grasshopper's commercial training market is large and explicitly aimed at non-programmers: Think Parametric's "Grasshopper 101" is "for people with no parametric design background" (interface, data management, basic programming concepts) — [Think Parametric](https://www.thinkparametric.com/courses/grasshopper-101-introduction-to-parametric-modelling); a Udemy "beginner to advanced" course covering lists, trees and vectors has c. 5,800 students — [Udemy](https://www.udemy.com/course/parametric-design-with-grasshopper-full-beginner-to-advanced/); Archademia pitches to "little or no prior experience" and builds to façade/pavilion projects — [Archademia](https://archademia.com/lessons/parametric-architecture-course-guide-to-grasshopper/); Parametric House — [Paracourse](https://parametrichouse.com/paracourse/)

### Inferences
- Of the architecture tools, Rhino is the only one where the student artefact, licence and program are literally the professional ones (perpetual, same binary, commercially usable, US$195 vs US$995). Everyone else gives students the professional program but with a time fuse (Autodesk: one year, renewable), a watermark (Archicad), or a non-commercial clause (Vectorworks, Autodesk). The beginner's Revit or Archicad model does live in the same formal system as the professional's; the discontinuity is contractual, not structural.
- Grasshopper is the best-documented case of a low floor that opens upward because its ceiling is studied, not assumed: Celani & Vaz show novices do better with it than with text, and the same literature plus Davis's thesis name the ceiling (perceptual limits of large visual graphs; brittle models under change). The escape hatch (scripting components and, in Rhino 8/9, Grasshopper 2) is inside the same canvas, so the move from "simple" to "expert" is a change of notation, not of tool. The claim that Grasshopper contains C#/Python script components is standard knowledge but was not verified in this session.
- Dynamo reproduces the same ladder inside BIM (nodes → Code Blocks/DesignScript → Python/.NET), which is the clearest "progressive disclosure" design in the AEC field, and it is the pattern a fashion tool could borrow: one data model, several notations of increasing power.
- SketchUp's plateau is partly manufactured: the free product is a different product (web, no extensions, no LayOut, no DWG), so the "ceiling" a hobbyist hits is a paywall between editions rather than a limit of the modelling kernel. Its per-face mesh representation (not a B-rep or NURBS kernel) is the structural limit for product or soft-goods work, but that characterisation is from background knowledge and was not sourced here.

Grasshopper's bundling history (included since Rhino 6, 2018) was not verified.
- No current (2026) Autodesk page for Revit specifically was reachable; the one-year renewable term is from Autodesk's general Education pages.
- Archicad's underlying geometry kernel and GDL scripting language, and Revit's family/parameter data model and its constraint solver, were not researched (search budget exhausted); treat any statement about them as unsourced.
- The Nexus Network Journal "Design of Dataflow" paper and the Open House International "incomplete recipes" paper were seen by title/abstract only.

## Key Question 2 — Product and mechanical: Onshape, Fusion, SolidWorks, FreeCAD, OpenSCAD, CadQuery/build123d, Creo and NX. Kernels and constraint solvers; how a beginner enters; what breaks.

### Takeaway
Onshape is the cleanest mechanical example of "same system from novice to enterprise": one cloud program, Parasolid + D-Cubed underneath, with the free and education tiers differing only in document privacy and add-on modules. Fusion is free for hobbyists and students but gates the hobby tier by document count and export formats; SolidWorks (Parasolid) became free for students on 1 July 2026; FreeCAD 1.1 (March 2026, Open CASCADE) and the Python code-CAD libraries (CadQuery, build123d, also Open CASCADE) give an open, exact, scriptable ladder whose main cost is kernel fragility; OpenSCAD is the lowest floor of the code tools but outputs meshes, which is what breaks when people try to take it into manufacturing. Creo (Granite) and NX (Parasolid) are the far ceiling and both have free student editions whose files cannot cross into the commercial product.

### Cited Findings

Kernels and constraint solvers (shared infrastructure)
- Parasolid was developed by Shape Data Ltd and is now owned and developed by Siemens Digital Industries Software; Siemens' Cambridge (UK) components group is responsible for Parasolid and D-Cubed; Siemens uses them in its own NX and Solid Edge and licenses them to "practically every CAD company"; SOLIDWORKS and Onshape "both use the Parasolid kernel" — [engineering.com, "Parasolid, D-Cubed and Siemens: The Heart of Your CAD Software Belongs to Another"](https://www.engineering.com/parasolid-d-cubed-and-siemens-the-heart-of-your-cad-software-belongs-to-another/); background on kernels — [engineering.com, "What is a geometric modeling kernel?"](https://www.engineering.com/what-is-a-geometric-modeling-kernel/)
- Siemens lists its PLM Components customers as "Autodesk, Ansys, Bentley, the Nemetschek Group and Solidworks, as well as start-ups like Onshape, Shapr3D and SimScale" — [Siemens PLM Components, Parasolid](https://www.siemens.com/en-us/products/plm-components/parasolid/); D-Cubed components: parametric sketching (2D DCM), assembly constraints (3D DCM), offset profiles (PGM), motion (AEM), collision detection (CDM), hidden line (HLM) — [CIMdata whitepaper on Siemens PLM Components (PDF)](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/10/2023/05/CIMdata_Whitepaper_Siemens_PLM_Components.pdf)

- PTC's Granite was PTC's in-house kernel for Pro/ENGINEER (named around 1998 per a forum anecdote); "Granite One" was offered as a licensable kernel in 2001 so third parties could keep associativity with Pro/E — [Design News, "Granite is foundation for interoperability and associativity"](https://www.designnews.com/motion-control/granite-is-foundation-for-interoperability-and-associativity); Autodesk CFD documentation calls Granite "the fundamental geometry kernel in Pro/Engineer" — [Autodesk CFD help](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2023/ENU/SimCFD-UsersGuide/files/GUID-5B7A4A74-02F0-46E2-9D31-4F738104DA84.htm); a CAD-interoperability vendor claims Granite "has been superseded by ACIS in more recent Creo versions" — [CADInterop, Granite format page](https://www.cadinterop.com/en/formats/neutral-format/granite.html) — this conflicts with PTC's own positioning and is probably a conflation with Creo Elements/Direct (ex-CoCreate, ACIS-based); treat as unreliable

### Inferences
- Structural continuity ranking (mechanical): Onshape > SolidWorks (post-July-2026 free student Standard) ≈ Fusion (education) > Fusion personal > FreeCAD/CadQuery/build123d (continuous but with a fragile kernel) > OpenSCAD (continuous as a language, discontinuous as geometry) > NX/Creo student editions (same program, but files are quarantined from the commercial product). Onshape ranks first because the free-tier model is the same Parasolid/D-Cubed document a Fortune-500 team would open; the only change on upgrade is privacy and modules.
- What breaks on the consumer-to-professional move is rarely geometry and usually one of: (a) export/format gates (Fusion personal lost DWG/DXF/IGES/SAT; STEP survived only after protest), (b) document-count or privacy gates (Fusion's 10 documents; Onshape free is public), (c) file quarantine (NX student files; Archicad's watermark), (d) the mental model, not the file: Tinkercad's primitives-only CSG becomes editable in Fusion only if built from primitives, and students report the real hurdle is learning to "think in 2D before moving to 3D" (sketch-then-feature), i.e. the constraint-solver paradigm itself.
- OpenSCAD is Lean-like (declarative, exact as a language) but its geometric output is a mesh, so the formal system a beginner learns does not carry the manufacturing semantics (B-rep faces, fillets, STEP) a professional needs. build123d/CadQuery fix this by sitting on OCCT, at the price of a harder install and a less forgiving API (the HN "once you get past its setup" remark and the 3–4x LLM error rate are the same cost seen twice). For a fashion analogue: a textual pattern language must keep the professional's geometry (true 3D surfaces, seam allowances as B-rep offsets) or it will plateau the way OpenSCAD does.
- The kernel map is relevant to "self-consistency": Parasolid+D-Cubed (Onshape, SolidWorks, NX, Solid Edge, Vectorworks), ShapeManager/ACIS lineage (Inventor, and by widespread report Fusion), Granite (Creo), OCCT (FreeCAD, CadQuery, build123d). A beginner who learns sketch constraints in any Parasolid/D-Cubed tool is literally using the same solver as the professional; the kernels differ in robustness (OCCT's fillet/boolean fragility) more than in expressiveness.

## Key Question 4 — Pedagogy and evidence: low floor/high ceiling/wide walls, parametric design education, the novice-to-expert path in CAD, progressive disclosure, and why some tools plateau

### Takeaway
The "low floor, high ceiling" phrase originates with Papert's Logo work in the 1970s; Resnick (with Silverman) added "wide walls" and has said Scratch deliberately traded the high ceiling for a low floor and wide walls, which is exactly the trade consumer CAD (Tinkercad, SketchUp Free) makes. The CAD-specific evidence is thin but consistent: novices do better in visual dataflow than in text (Celani & Vaz), visual graphs are "perceptually limited" at scale, parametric models break under change (Davis), and the documented novice barriers are the sketch-constraint mental model and spatial reasoning rather than tool features. Aish & Woodbury's "multi-level interaction" (2005) is the earliest explicit argument for what the parent report calls progressive disclosure in design tools.

### Cited Findings
- Origin and extension: the metaphor "was first conceived by Seymour Papert in the 1970s as a design principle for the Logo programming language"; Resnick's addition, "wide walls", means a tool must "support multiple pathways from floor to ceiling"; Resnick has said that when Scratch faced a choice his group "focused on low floor and wide walls, and compromised on the high ceiling" — [Gordon Brander, "Low floor, wide walls, high ceiling"](https://gordonbrander.com/notes/low-floor-wide-walls-high-ceiling/); [Nikki C., "The architecture of creativity"](https://nikkic.substack.com/p/the-architecture-of-creativity-low); [John Umekubo, "Low Floors, High Ceilings, and Wide Walls"](https://johnumekubo.com/2017/11/09/low-floors-high-ceilings-and-wide-walls/); [Chobitto, workshop-design principles](https://www.chobitto.jp/en/blog/1529) (secondary blogs; they disagree on whether Resnick coined "wide walls" alone or with Brian Silverman)
- Primary text: Resnick & Silverman, "Some reflections on designing construction kits for kids" (IDC 2005) — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/247589124_Some_reflections_on_designing_construction_kits_for_kids) (record retrieved, full text not read in this session)
- The framework is used as a tool-evaluation lens: one note observes that "most programming tools prioritize the high ceiling"; another applies it to Tinkercad, arguing that "it's what you create in this environment that provides the wide walls" — among the blog sources above (attribution to a specific blog not confirmed); an education-technology paper notes the concepts "have traditionally been used in TUI design for computing, [but] they are also useful in geometry teaching" — [TIEboard, arXiv 2505.21891](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.21891); the framework is also applied to novice AI maker kits — [arXiv 1805.10723](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.10723) and to lab-automation interfaces — [arXiv 2606.10182](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.10182)
- Visual vs textual in design education: Celani & Vaz (2012) — novices better with visual programming; advanced students needed scripting for rule-based generative systems — [CumInCAD](https://papers.cumincad.org/cgi-bin/works/paper/ijac201210108); the "incomplete recipes" teaching paper summarises the comparative studies as showing visual algorithm construction is "perceptually limited" and "more successful in relatively simple and pedagogical uses" — [Emerald / Open House International](https://www.emerald.com/ohi/article/49/4/736/1212363/Teaching-parametric-design-fostering-algorithmic); a Springer chapter on declarative visual programming for lattice design cites Celani & Vaz as the basis for teaching visual programming to designers without programming background — [Springer, 978-3-031-47372-2_15](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-47372-2_15)
- Multi-level interaction / progressive disclosure: Aish & Woodbury (2005) argue designers work at two levels (defining schemata and constraints; searching instances) and need multiple views of the same constraint model — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/11536482_13)
- Why parametric models plateau or break: Davis (2013) — models cannot absorb some changes; rebuild-or-abandon; no agreed measure of flexibility; remedies borrowed from software engineering (structured programming, interactive programming, language paradigms) — [danieldavis.com/thesis](https://danieldavis.com/thesis)
- Novice barriers in parametric CAD: parametric software "challenges novice users due to the complex user interfaces, steep learning curve, and the need for strong spatial understanding to manipulate 3D models" (AR parametric-modelling study, n = 8) — [ASME JCISE, "pARametric"](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/computingengineering/article/25/4/041001/1212230/pARametric-Empowering-In-Situ-Parametric-Modeling)
- Expertise in parametric modelling is about strategy, not commands: Camba, Contero & Company studied engineering students with prior SolidWorks experience who "received no formal training in modeling methodologies" but were instructed to build reusable models with design intent, comparing methodologies on models of rising complexity — [ResearchGate, "Parametric CAD modeling: An analysis of strategies for design reusability"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291553286_Parametric_CAD_modeling_An_analysis_of_strategies_for_design_reusability); [PDF mirror](https://scispace.com/pdf/parametric-cad-modeling-3ufg84xybx.pdf)
- Complexity perception: expert ratings of parametric model complexity show a linear growth zone and a plateau zone covering about 10% of models; the authors propose using the measure to sequence "modeling exercises of increasing complexity based on the student's performance level" — [Advanced Engineering Informatics (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1474034623000988)
- Teaching novices CAD quality: a paper proposes rubrics "to enforce quality modeling during the CAD training of novice product designers" (via the same result set; title-level only) — [Engineering LibreTexts, Parametric CAD chapter](https://eng.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_Engineering/Introduction_to_Engineering_-_Thinking_Like_an_Engineer/12:_Parametric_CAD_and_3D_Modeling)
- Consumer-to-pro transition anecdotes (Tinkercad → Fusion): students found the switch hard because "we had to think in 2D before moving to 3D" — [JMU course blog](https://sites.lib.jmu.edu/isci104-spring2020/?p=2589); hobbyists cite the primitives-only limit of Tinkercad's export to Fusion — [Western Thunder forum](https://westernthunder.me.uk/threads/smoothing-curved-output-from-tinkercad.12541); hobby forums repeatedly name Fusion's 10-document cap as the main pain point and note full editable history cannot be kept past it — [V1 Engineering forum](https://forum.v1engineering.com/t/fusion-360-changes/20975?page=4)

### Inferences
- The literature supports a sharper version of the parent thesis: tools plateau when the beginner's representation is not the expert's (Tinkercad primitives, SketchUp faces, OpenSCAD meshes) or when the licence quarantines the beginner's files (NX Student, Archicad watermark, Fusion personal exports). Tools keep opening up when the representation is shared and the notation ladder lives inside one environment (Grasshopper → scripting; Dynamo nodes → Code Blocks → Python; Blender modifiers → Geometry Nodes → Python; Onshape sketches → FeatureScript, the last from background knowledge).
- Resnick's own admission that Scratch sacrificed the high ceiling is the honest version of what consumer CAD does; the design question for fashion is whether to accept that trade (Tinkercad model) or to follow the Rhino/Onshape/Blender model where the floor is lowered by tutorials and defaults rather than by a reduced product.
- Aish & Woodbury (2005) and Davis (2013) together give a research-grounded definition of "high ceiling" in design tools: not more features, but the ability of a model to survive change (flexibility) and to be viewed at several levels (schema, instance, code). That is a usable criterion for the fashion report.
- The pedagogical evidence base is small (one controlled comparison from 2012, one PhD by reflective practice, an n = 8 AR study, a few CAD-education rubric papers); claims about "progressive disclosure" in CAD are mostly design arguments rather than measured outcomes.

### Gaps
- Resnick et al., "Scratch: Programming for All" (CACM 2009) and Resnick's book *Lifelong Kindergarten* were not retrieved (search budget exhausted); the Scratch "compromised on the high ceiling" quote is from secondary blogs.
- Bret Victor, "Learnable Programming" (2012) could not be fetched; none of its principles are cited here.
- Leitão, Santos & Lopes, "Programming languages for generative design: a comparative study" (IJAC 2012), which argues visual dataflow does not scale and compares Grasshopper with textual languages, was not retrieved; the "incomplete recipes" paper's summary of "both of these studies" likely refers to it, but that is my reading.
- No study specifically on Tinkercad or SketchUp learning plateaus was found; the plateau evidence is anecdotal (forums, course blogs). No eCAADe/CAADRIA paper on Grasshopper learning curves with measured outcomes was located.

- The crossover follows the kernel: fashion's hard-goods neighbours (jewellery, heels, soles, lasts, hardware) have moved to Rhino because free-form NURBS surfaces plus Grasshopper's graded variants fit those objects; soft goods (bags, apparel) have not moved to any of these general tools because none of them represents a flat pattern with seam allowances and grain as a first-class object (that is the dedicated-tool territory other researchers cover).
- What fashion could borrow, by tool: from Rhino/Grasshopper, the single perpetual licence from student to studio and the nodes-then-script ladder; from Onshape, "same document in free, student and enterprise, differing only in privacy and modules"; from Blender, that a free tool can be the production tool if the community supplies the domain layer (sewing toolboxes) on top of a general procedural language; from Dynamo, three notations over one data model; from p5.js, managed migration so novices are not stranded by version breaks.
- The absence of Onshape/Fusion/FreeCAD in soft goods is probably structural (B-rep solids are the wrong primitive for cloth) rather than a licensing issue; surface-flattening add-ons (e.g., ExactFlat for SolidWorks/Rhino) exist but were not researched here (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- ExactFlat and other soft-goods flattening tools for Rhino/SolidWorks, MatrixGold's relationship to Rhino (jewellery), and Grasshopper plug-ins for knit or textile structures were not researched (search budget exhausted).
- No primary evidence on which CAD tools Nike, adidas or any named bag/apparel brand uses; the footwear evidence is course- and reseller-level.
- Blender's use inside fashion companies (as opposed to add-on availability) and any Houdini use in soft goods were not found.

## Key Question 6 — Classification (Lean-like vs Mathematica-like) and the continuity test applied to each environment

### Takeaway
Classified on the "formal, programmatic, exact" vs "interactive, accessible, still principled" axis and then scored on whether the beginner's artefact is the professional's: Grasshopper, Dynamo, OpenSCAD, CadQuery/build123d and Houdini are Lean-like; Rhino, Onshape, Fusion, SolidWorks, FreeCAD, Blender, Revit/Archicad/Vectorworks and SketchUp are Mathematica-like. The tools that pass the continuity test outright are Rhino+Grasshopper, Onshape, Blender, FreeCAD and the Python code-CAD libraries; Fusion, SolidWorks (student), Revit/Archicad/Vectorworks pass structurally but fail contractually; SketchUp Free, Tinkercad, OpenSCAD (as geometry) and the NX/Creo student editions fail structurally or by quarantine.

### Inferences
- The distinction the parent report wants is cleaner as two axes than as one: (1) representation continuity (is the beginner's artefact the professional's object?) and (2) notation ladder (is there a path from direct manipulation to nodes to code inside the same environment?). Rhino+Grasshopper, Onshape (+FeatureScript), Blender (+Geometry Nodes), Revit (+Dynamo) and FreeCAD (+Python) satisfy both; OpenSCAD and p5.js satisfy (2) trivially but (1) only within their own output type; SketchUp and Tinkercad satisfy neither at the free tier.
- The licensing pattern that best matches "same system, elementary use" is Rhino's (one perpetual program, two prices) and Onshape's (one cloud program, privacy as the paywall); the pattern most likely to produce a plateau is Autodesk's and Trimble's (a reduced hobby product next to the professional one), and the Siemens/Graphisoft pattern (full program, quarantined files) produces a cliff at graduation rather than a plateau.
- For a fashion tool, the implication is that the "kernel" to be shared between novice and expert should be the pattern-plus-3D-surface model itself, with the beginner given defaults and templates rather than a different file type; the price ladder can follow Rhino or Onshape, and the notation ladder can follow Grasshopper/Dynamo.

### Gaps
- Classification entries marked "unconfirmed here" rest on background knowledge: Rhino's in-house NURBS kernel, SketchUp's face-based representation, Fusion's use of ShapeManager, Onshape's FeatureScript, Blender's Python API, Houdini Apprentice's format/resolution limits, FreeCAD 1.0's toponaming fix. None was verifiable in this session because vendor sites were proxy-blocked and the search budget was exhausted.
- Archicad's and Revit's internal geometry kernels and Revit's constraint solver were not researched.
